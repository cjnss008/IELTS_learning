Reading the Numbers Carefully

For a class project, Ben compared two fictional shopping centres. A chart showed that visits to Centre A rose from forty thousand to fifty thousand in one year. This was an increase of twenty-five per cent. Ben first wrote that visits had risen to twenty-five per cent, but his teacher asked him to check the units.

Centre B experienced a gradual decline before reaching a plateau. Its total revenue remained fairly stable because average spending increased. Ben learned that a trend in one measure did not necessarily describe the whole business. He needed an overview before listing individual figures.

The proportion of visitors arriving by bicycle also changed. At Centre A, it rose from ten per cent to fifteen per cent, a rise of five percentage points. In relative terms, that was a fifty per cent increase. The two statements described the same change from different perspectives.

Ben revised his report and removed unsupported explanations. The chart did not reveal why customers behaved differently, so he avoided inventing a cause. He compared the most noticeable features and checked every percentage. His final paragraph was shorter, but the figures were easier to understand and the comparisons were more precise.
